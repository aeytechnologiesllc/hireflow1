In October 2026, the people behind HireFlow needed a team lead for the chat support team of a US online business they run. We hired through HireFlow, the same way any business on it would.

The numbers: **155 people applied, 147 of them in the first four days. 74 finished every step. Three stood out enough for a call, and we hired from those three.**

This is how it went, step by step, and what we would do the same way next time.

## The job

This was a working team lead role. The lead answers customers on their own shifts, just like the team, and leads a team of six chat agents: coaching them, reviewing their chats, taking the hardest conversations, and making sure everyone follows the business's rules on money and accounts.

The post said plainly what the job was not: "if you are looking for a role where you only manage other people, this isn't it." That one line is worth putting in any post: it tells people who want a desk job managing others, before they apply, that this is not it.

Other things the post made clear before anyone applied:

- **Chat only.** No phone calls, ever.
- **One fixed shift,** written in Philippine time and in US Eastern time, with a note that the US time moves by an hour when the clocks change.
- **Full time,** five days a week, with the occasional cover for a teammate.
- **Every step online,** listed in order, so people knew what they were signing up for.
- **A promise:** everyone who finishes every step gets a yes or no by email.

## Where we posted

We posted on a Philippine job board, [OnlineJobs.ph](https://www.onlinejobs.ph), and sent everyone to the same application link. Applications started the next morning, and most came in the first four days.

If you post in more than one place, keep that rule: one link for everyone. Every applicant then goes through the same steps, and you have one list to read instead of several inboxes.

## What we asked for

For this lead, four things mattered most:

1. **Getting the answer right.** The rules on money are strict, and a wrong answer costs more than a slow one.
2. **Calm under pressure.** The lead takes the conversations the team cannot finish, and those are the angry ones.
3. **Coaching.** Reviewing an agent's chat and explaining, kindly and clearly, what to do differently.
4. **Handling change.** The business's tools and rules change often. The lead has to learn the new way fast and bring the team along without drama.

## How 155 became three

Every applicant went through the same steps, in the same order:

1. **A short form** with a few questions about their experience and the shift.
2. **A skills check:** short situations a team lead meets every week, each one timed.
3. **A check of their computer and connection,** because a remote lead with a bad connection cannot lead.
4. **A chat practice** with a simulated customer who is already upset. This is the job's hardest moment, in miniature.
5. **A written interview,** answered on a computer.

We did not read 155 résumés. We read the ranked results and started at the top.

What made the strongest few stand out was not their résumé. It was how specific they were. One of them described a time a process changed at work: they learned the new process, tested it, wrote a short guide for the team, held a team huddle to walk everyone through it, and then watched the first days closely. That answer is the job, described from the inside. Vague answers ("I am a good leader and a fast learner") told us nothing, however polished they were.

## The calls

Each call was booked for 30 minutes on video. By then the written steps had already shown how each person works, so the call was for what writing cannot show: how they talk a problem through, and the questions on both sides about the shift, the start date and the first weeks.

If you want a list of questions and what to listen for, there is one in [chat support interview questions](/blog/chat-support-interview-questions).

## What we would do the same way again

- **Test before you read.** The ranked results pointed us to the right people quickly. Reading 155 applications first would have taken days, and the best answers would have looked much like the rest on paper.
- **Weigh the chat practice most.** Of every step, the live chat with an upset customer told us the most about how someone will really work. It is also the hardest step to fake.
- **Say what the job is not.** One honest line in the post did more filtering than any question.
- **Keep the steps short, and say how long they take.** People finish what they can plan for.
- **Answer everyone.** A yes or a no by email, for everyone who finished. People who gave you an hour deserve an answer.

There is more on the method in [screening 150 applicants without reading 150 résumés](/blog/screening-150-applicants-without-reading-150-resumes), and on the tests themselves in [five chat tests to give before hiring a support agent](/blog/five-chat-tests-before-hiring-a-support-agent).

## In short

- Post where your applicants already look, with one link for everyone.
- Write the shift in their time zone and yours.
- Say plainly what the job is, and what it is not.
- Give everyone the same short, real test, with a live chat at its center.
- Read the strongest few closely, and call the best three.
- Answer everyone who finished.
