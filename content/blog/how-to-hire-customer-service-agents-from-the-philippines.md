Post a clear job on Philippine job boards and LinkedIn. Write your hours in Philippine time as well as your own. Give every applicant the same short work sample built from your own customer situations, call the best few, and agree the pay, the currency and the payment method in writing before anyone starts. The rest of this guide covers each step, and the mistakes to avoid.

## Where to post

Start with Philippine job boards and LinkedIn. You can also ask people you already work with for referrals, and add the job to your own website if you have a careers page.

A few habits make your post easier to find and easier to understand:

- Use the job title people actually search for. "Customer Service Representative (Chat)" or "Phone Support Agent" is easier to find than a title only your company uses.
- Put "remote" or "work from home" in the title if the job is remote.
- Name the channel in the title: chat, phone, email, or a mix.

If you post in several places, send everyone to the same application link. That way every applicant goes through the same steps, and you have one list to review instead of several.

## Time zones

Philippine time is UTC+8, and the Philippines does not use daylight saving time. The clock there never moves forward or back.

That matters if your own clock does. When you change your clocks, the gap between you and your agent changes by an hour, even though their working hours stay the same on their side.

Here is a worked example for a business on UTC-5 in standard time and UTC-4 in daylight saving time:

| Your time | During your standard time (UTC-5) | During your daylight saving time (UTC-4) |
|---|---|---|
| 9 a.m. | 10 p.m. Philippine time | 9 p.m. Philippine time |
| 5 p.m. | 6 a.m. Philippine time | 5 a.m. Philippine time |

What to do about it:

- Write the shift in both time zones in your post, for example: "9 a.m. to 5 p.m. our time (UTC-5), which is 10 p.m. to 6 a.m. Philippine time."
- Say whether the hours shift by an hour during the year, and when.
- If the shift falls at night in the Philippines, say so plainly. Some people want night work. Others cannot do it. Saying it up front saves everyone time.
- Plan a few hours of overlap in the first weeks, when you and the new agent are both awake, for training and questions.
- Agree which holidays the agent takes. Philippine holidays will not always match yours.

## What to put in the post

A good post answers the questions a serious applicant has before they apply. Include:

- **The business.** What you do and who your customers are. For example: "A family dental office. You will help patients book, move and ask about appointments."
- **The channel.** Chat, phone, email, or a mix. For chat, how many conversations at once.
- **The hours and days,** in both time zones, and whether the job is full time or part time.
- **The tools.** The help desk, chat or calendar software they will use. General terms are fine.
- **Private details.** What they will see, such as patient records or order and payment details, and how you expect them to handle it.
- **Equipment.** Their own computer, a steady internet connection, a headset for phone work, a quiet place to work, and a plan for when the power or internet goes out.
- **The steps.** What the application involves and roughly how long it takes, so people can set aside the time.

Leave out anything about age. Lines such as "fresh graduates only" or "below 30" can break Philippine law. There is more on that below.

## How to screen

Give every applicant the same steps, in the same order:

1. **A short application form.** Contact details and work history. Nothing asking for age or birth date (see the age rule below).
2. **A connection check for remote roles.** Are their internet and computer good enough for the work?
3. **A work sample.** A few real customer messages from your kind of business, including one hard one. Time it if the job is live chat.
4. **A few written questions.** For phone roles, ask how they would handle specific calls.
5. **A short call with the best three to five.** For phone roles, this is where you hear how they speak.

Score every answer with the same simple scorecard: did they solve the problem, was the tone right, was the English clear, and did they follow your rules. Then read only the top few closely. ## Paying people

Before the person starts, agree three things in writing: the pay, the currency, and the payment method.

## The age rule

A Philippine law, Republic Act No. 10911, makes it unlawful for an employer to require a declaration of age or birth date during the application process, or to publish a job notice that suggests an age preference.

If you must confirm that someone is an adult, ask your lawyer whether a yes-or-no question such as "Are you 18 or older?" fits your role. Collect the exact date of birth after the hire, from the ID or papers you ask for then.

You can read more in [what you can't ask Filipino applicants](/blog/what-you-cannot-ask-filipino-applicants). This is general information, not legal advice. Check with a lawyer for your own case.

## The first two weeks

A good hire still needs a good start. Before day one:

- Set up access to every tool they need, and test it together.
- Write down the answers to your most common customer questions.
- Share a few past conversations that went well, with private details removed, and explain why they worked.
- Plan to read their first conversations with them, and give feedback early and kindly.

## In short

- Post on Philippine job boards and LinkedIn, with a clear title that names the channel.
- Philippine time is UTC+8 with no daylight saving. Write your hours in both time zones.
- Say plainly if the shift is at night in the Philippines.
- Describe the business, the channel, the hours, the tools, the private details and the equipment.
- Give everyone the same steps, and score them the same way.
- Call the best three to five.
- Agree the pay, the currency and the payment method in writing.
- Never ask for age or birth date while people apply.
