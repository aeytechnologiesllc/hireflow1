A good front-desk agent keeps your schedule full and your patients calm. A careless one can give medical advice, promise what insurance will pay, or tell a stranger about a patient. Hire for the first. Test hard for the second.

This guide is for an agent who answers your patients by phone, chat, email or text, in your office or remotely.

## What the job is

Most of the work is the schedule.

- **Booking.** The right provider, the right kind of visit, the right length of slot.
- **Moving and canceling.** Finding a new time that works, and filling the gap left behind.
- **Reminders.** Contacting patients before visits, confirming, and handling "I can't make it."
- **Everyday questions.** Hours, address, parking, what to bring, how to fill out forms.
- **Messages.** Getting each question to the right person: a nurse, the dentist, billing.
- **New patients.** Taking down details correctly the first time.

On a good day, there are few empty slots, patients know when to come and what to bring, and every clinical question reaches a clinical person quickly.

## What they must never do

These three rules matter more than speed or charm. Put them in the job post, in training and in your tests.

### Never give medical advice

Patients will ask things like "Is this swelling normal after my filling?" or "Can I take my medicine before the blood test?" The agent may want to help. They must not answer.

Their job is to get the question to a clinical person fast, and to give your emergency instruction when it applies. A good reply sounds like this: "I can't advise on that, but I'm sending your question to the nurse now. The nurse will call you back this afternoon."

Ask your clinical team for two things the agent can use word for word: a short list of words and symptoms that always go straight to a clinician, and your office's emergency instruction.

### Never promise what insurance will pay

"Will my insurance cover this?" comes up often. The agent can say which plans you accept and what the patient should ask their insurer. They must not promise coverage or a final price unless your office has confirmed it.

- Good: "We do accept that plan. What it pays depends on your plan, so it's worth checking with them. I can send you the details of the visit to ask about."
- Weak: "Yes, that's covered, don't worry."

A wrong promise here turns into a billing dispute later, and the patient will remember who made it.

### Never share patient details with the wrong person

Your agent will get requests like these:

- "I'm calling for my mother. What time is her appointment?"
- "I'm her husband. What did the doctor say?"
- An email asking about a patient, from an address you do not have on file.

The agent must check who they are speaking with, using your office's rules, before sharing anything. If the person is not allowed to know, the agent shares nothing and stays kind: "I'm not able to share that, but I can take a message for her, or she can call us to say you may speak for her."

Your rules may also cover voicemails, text reminders and what can be said where others can hear. Write them down. An agent cannot follow rules that live only in your head.

A note on the law: what you must do with patient information depends on where you work and which laws apply to you. This guide is general information, not legal advice. Check with a lawyer for your own case.

## What to test

Test before you interview, using situations from your own office. Give every applicant the same tests.

1. **A booking with a catch.** A patient wants the earliest slot with a certain dentist, but needs a longer visit than that slot allows. Give a simple sample schedule. Do they pick a slot that fits, or squeeze the patient in?
2. **A medical question.** "I had a tooth pulled yesterday and it's still bleeding a little. Is that okay?" A good answer gives no advice, passes the question to a clinician quickly, and gives the emergency instruction. A weak answer says "That's normal" and moves on.
3. **The insurance question.** "Will my plan cover a crown?" Look for a helpful answer that promises nothing.
4. **The wrong person.** "Hi, I'm calling for my husband. Can you tell me what the doctor said at his visit?" A good answer is warm, follows your rules, and shares nothing it should not.
5. **The upset patient.** Their appointment was moved twice, or they waited a long time. Look for a calm apology and a real next step, not a list of excuses.
6. **Two patients at once.** For chat roles, run two conversations together. A detail from one patient must never appear in the other patient's chat.

For phone roles, ask a few written questions about calls first. Then run a short practice call with the people you pick, where you play the patient. Tests 2 and 4 make good scripts.

For remote roles, check the setup: a quiet, private room, a stable internet connection, and a computer that can run your scheduling and phone tools. "Private" matters here. Patient names should not be overheard.

## A short job description outline

Use this as a starting point and fill in your own details.

- **Title:** Front-desk agent, dental office (phone and chat, remote)
- **About us:** your kind of practice, how many providers you have, and who your patients are.
- **What you will do:** book, move and cancel appointments; send reminders; answer everyday questions; pass clinical and billing questions to the right person.
- **What you will never do:** give medical advice, promise what insurance will pay, or share patient details with anyone your rules do not allow.
- **Hours and time zone:** the exact shift, in your time zone and the applicant's.
- **Tools:** your scheduling system, phone system and chat tool, and whether you will train people on them.
- **Pay:** a number or a range, and how often you pay.
- **How we hire:** a short skills check, a practice patient conversation, a written interview, then a call.
- **How to apply:** one clear step.

If many of your applicants are in the Philippines, leave age and birth date out of the post and the application. ## In short

- The job is mostly the schedule: booking, moving, reminding, answering and passing messages on.
- Three rules come first: no medical advice, no insurance promises, no patient details to the wrong person.
- Write those rules down, with a word-for-word emergency instruction from your clinical team.
- Test with your own situations: a tricky booking, a medical question, an insurance question, the wrong caller, an upset patient and, for chat roles, two patients at once.
- For phone roles, add written questions about calls and a short practice call you run yourself.
- Leave age and birth date out of the post and the application.
